1. BASIC SIGNAL COMMUNICATION

📘 Words you need to know first

  • Signal communication: sending messages between ships or units by radio, flags, lights, or beeps.
  • Transmit: send a message (talk on the radio). Receive: get a message.
  • Station: anyone with a radio, like a ship or a unit. Think of it as "a person on the group chat."
  • Call sign: a station's code name on the radio (like a username).
  • Net (network): a group of stations talking on the same channel (like a group chat).
  • NCS (Net Control Station): the station in charge of the net (like the group chat admin).
  • Proword (procedure word): a fixed word with a fixed meaning on the radio, e.g. ROGER = "I got it."
  • Prosign (procedure sign): the same idea as a proword, but sent as letters by flag or light, e.g. K = "go ahead."
  • Precedence: how urgent a message is (its priority). Higher precedence = more urgent and goes first.
  • Radio silence: an order that nobody transmits (usually so the enemy can't detect you).
  • Semaphore: signaling with two hand-held flags. Each arm position = one letter.
  • Flashing light: signaling by blinking a light in Morse code.
  • Morse code: letters made of dots (short) and dashes (long).
  • Flag hoist: raising flags up a ship's pole (mast) to send a signal.
  • Receipt: confirmation that a message was received.
  • Acknowledgement: confirmation that a message was received, understood, and will be obeyed.

A. Voice Radio (talking on the radio)

  • The formal name is Radio Telephony. It's also called Voice Radio (VR).
  • It's used for short-range communication.
  • It works by line-of-sight: the radios must "see" each other with no big obstacle (like a mountain) between them.
  • It's commonly used during exercises in the area of operations (training or mission areas).

Microphone DOs

  • Listen before you talk. Cutting into someone else's message causes confusion and delay.
  • Speak clearly, slowly (a slow message gets through faster than a rushed one), and naturally, in your normal voice and with standard pronunciation.
  • Keep the mic 2 inches from your lips.
  • When someone asks for a radio check, tell them honestly how well you hear them.
  • Pause between parts of the message and follow the proper procedure.
  • Say what you need, then get off the air (stop transmitting so others can talk).

Microphone DON'Ts

  • Don't talk on the radio while people around you are noisy (discussing the next maneuver).
  • Don't press the mic button until you're ready to talk.
  • Don't send a test signal longer than 10 seconds.
  • Don't transmit without permission, and don't break radio silence.
  • Don't talk on a directed net without permission (see "Nets" below).
  • Don't say your real name or personal sign, and don't use unauthorized language (slang or curses).

C. Prowords to memorize (fixed radio words)

Proword Meaning in simple words
ROGER "I received your message." That's all it means.
WILCO "Will comply": I received it, understood it, and will do it
ACKNOWLEDGE "Tell me you received it and will do it" (the reply is WILCO)
SAY AGAIN "Repeat that, I missed some words"
CORRECTION "I made a mistake." Repeat the last correct word, then continue.
DISREGARD THIS TRANSMISSION "Ignore what I'm sending" (cancels a message while it's being sent)
RELAY "Pass this message on to everyone it's addressed to"
RELAY TO (+ name) "Pass this message on to that specific station"
EXEMPT "Everyone in the group except this station"
  • ⚠️ Never say "ROGER WILCO." WILCO already includes ROGER.
  • ⚠️ A message that was fully sent AND receipted can only be cancelled by sending another message. Saying "disregard" is too late.
  • A message is not considered delivered until a receipt (ROGER) is given.
  • Break-in: a station with a more urgent (higher precedence) message can interrupt an ongoing transmission. During tactical (combat) messages this is not allowed, EXCEPT to report enemy contact.

D. Semaphore (two-flag signaling)

What it is: a signalman holds two flags and moves their arms into positions. Each position = one letter.

  • The flags: about 15–18 inches square, similar to the OSCAR flag (half red, half yellow). The PAPA flag is sometimes used instead. Each flag is on a stick long enough to grip firmly.
  • Why use semaphore?
    1. It's faster than flashing light.
    2. It may be more secure than flashing light, because it's short range and the enemy far away can't see it.
    3. It's the best substitute for routine (administrative) messages when radio silence is ordered.

When sending

  • Stand against a good background (so the flags are easy to see) and face the ship you're signaling.
  • Pause at each letter. Pause twice as long for prosigns, signals and abbreviations.
  • Between words, or when a letter repeats (like the "LL" in "BALL"), drop your arms to the FRONT position (arms down in front).
  • Numbers are spelled out (e.g. "FIVE"). To record them as digits, send the numeral sign before and after.

When calling a ship

  • Send the ship's call sign and the ATTENTION sign.
  • If calling by light, send SEM, which means "a semaphore message is coming."
  • If calling by flag hoist, raise flag JULIET to mean "semaphore message for all ships."
  • To show priority, raise DESIG below JULIET for a priority (urgent) message.

When answering

  • By semaphore: send the answering sign. By light: send K.
  • If the call was by flag hoist:
    • Answer flag raised halfway (at the dip) = "I see your signal"
    • Raised all the way (close up) = "I'm ready to receive"
    • Lowered (hauled down) = "Message received"

Asking for a repeat or interrupting

  • IMI = "please repeat"
  • AS = "wait" (to interrupt)

The semaphore wheel ("circles")

Circle Letters How
1st A B C D E F G A–C with the right arm, E–G with the left arm, D with either
2nd H I J K L M N The right arm stays at A, the left arm moves

  • Keep your arms straight when moving between positions.

F. International Morse Code

  • The standard code for navy messages sent by flashing light or radiotelegraph (a radio that sends beeps instead of voice).
  • Timing (1 unit = the length of one dot):
    • Dot = 1 unit
    • Dash = 3 units
    • Gap inside a letter = 1 unit
    • Gap between letters = 3 units
    • Gap between words = 7 units
  • 36 basic patterns: 26 letters + 10 numbers, plus punctuation.
  • Best way to learn: "by wholes" (memorize each letter's whole sound or pattern, not dot by dot). Practice code groups first, then plain words.

  • E (one dot) and T (one dash) are the shortest letters.

  • Every number has 5 symbols.

  • Core values: Honor. Dedication. Patriotism.

2. BASIC SURVIVAL

📘 Words you need to know

  • Evade / evader: hiding from or escaping the enemy, and the person doing it.
  • Snare: a loop of string or wire (noose) that tightens around an animal.
  • Trap: a device that closes or falls when the animal touches it.
  • Tinder: very dry, easy-to-light material (dry grass, paper). Kindling: small sticks that catch fire from the tinder.
  • Green stick: a fresh, still-alive stick that doesn't burn easily.
  • Water still: a hole in the ground covered with plastic that collects water through evaporation.
  • Succulent plants: thick, juicy plants that store water.
  • Quart: about 1 liter (one canteen).

SURVIVAL = staying alive in the field with limited resources. You may need it when you're separated from your unit, evading the enemy, or a prisoner.

  • Size up: consider your own capability, your surroundings, the totality of the situation, and your equipment and supplies
  • Undue haste: plan your moves deliberately or you waste energy and resources. Keep calm, take stock, and don't be too eager to move.
  • Remember where you are: know your position relative to enemy-controlled areas, friendly units, and water sources
  • Vanquish fear: hope for the best, but expect and prepare for the worst
  • Improvise: make do with available or indigenous materials, e.g. improvise a shelter
  • Value living: never lose hope. Preserve your health and conserve your strength. Your goal is to "get out alive."
  • Act like natives: learn how locals get food and water, and befriend them
  • Learn skills / Live by your wits: catching animals, building shelter

Jungle survival pointers

  • (a) Night comes early, so prepare for bed early while there's still light
  • (b) Be ready for sudden drops in temperature and don't sleep directly on the ground
  • (c) Protect yourself from insects: tuck trousers into boots, keep sleeves rolled down and buttoned
  • (d) Use clothing against scratches. Treat every wound with soap and water, ointment, and a clean dressing.
  • (e) Don't be bothered by strange sounds at night
  • (f) Avoid camping in depressions or low areas (valleys, dry riverbeds) because they flood

Food

  • Plant food: be knowledgeable about which plants or parts are edible. Ask the natives.
  • Animal food: has the most food value per unit weight. Anything that swims, flies, crawls or creeps is possible food.
    • All four-legged animals are normally edible. Cook them as soon as possible.
    • All eggs and insect larvae are edible
    • Most reptiles are edible, but watch out for poisonous toads
    • Snake: cut off 6 inches from the head
    • Seaweeds, crustaceans and mollusks are generally edible, but cook them
    • Almost all fish are edible. Use nets, fish traps, baskets, hooks and spears.

Traps and snares

  • Trapping is for the unarmed survivor or the evader who doesn't want to fire a rifle and be discovered
  • Snare = a noose that slips and strangles or holds the animal. Make it from parachute suspension lines, wire, or bark.
  • Trap = a contraption triggered by the prey's action (a door or gate closes)
  • No trap catches every animal. Look for trails, tracks, droppings, nests, and feeding/watering areas.
  • Trail vs run: a trail shows signs of use by several species
  • Types:
    • Treadle spring snare: small animals on a trail
    • Locking loop snare: tightens as the animal struggles
    • Drag noose snare: for medium-size animals, and lets you leave the site
    • Deadfall trap: a heavy log or rock falls on the game
  • Preserving food: if the situation and time allow, save the extra meat for later

Water

  • One of your most urgent needs. You need at least 2 quarts a day, even in cold areas. More than 3/4 of your body is fluid.
  • Purify: boil for at least 1 minute, OR add 8 drops of 2½% iodine per quart (a canteen-full) and wait 10 minutes
  • Rainwater caught directly in clean containers or on plants is generally safe without purifying
  • Locate sites for wells: especially important in the desert
  • Water-rich plants: succulents in tropical forest
  • Survival water still: dig where the soil holds moisture (dry stream bed or where rainwater collected), the soil is easy to dig, and sunlight hits most of the day

Shelter

  • Protects you from sun, insects, wind, rain, heat/cold, and enemy observation. In hostile areas it can matter more than food or water.
  • Site: safe from flooding and erosion, a pleasant place to live, near water
  • Things to consider:
    1. The time and effort needed to build it
    2. Whether it adequately protects you from the elements
    3. Whether the tools and materials are available (otherwise improvise)

Fire

  • Uses: keeps you warm, keeps you dry, cooks food, purifies water, signals
  • Choosing a site: the terrain and climate, materials and tools available, time you have, why you need the fire, and how near the enemy is
  • Preparing the site: a dry spot that is protected from the wind, placed well relative to your shelter, concentrates heat where you want it, and has wood nearby. Clear the brush and scrape the topsoil.

Fire lays

Method How
Tepee Tinder and kindling in a cone. Light the center. Burns well even with wet wood.
Lean-to Push a green stick into the ground at 30°, pointing in the direction of the wind. Put tinder under it and add kindling.
Cross-ditch Scratch a cross about 1 foot across, dig it 3 inches deep, put tinder in the middle and a kindling pyramid on top. The ditch makes a draft.

3. FIRST AID

📘 Words you need to know

  • Corpsman: a navy medic.
  • Shock: a dangerous condition where the body doesn't get enough blood flow (pale, cold, weak, fast pulse).
  • Tourniquet: a tight band around an arm or leg to stop severe bleeding (last resort only).
  • Dressing: the pad placed directly on a wound. Bandage: what holds the dressing in place.
  • PPE: Personal Protective Equipment (gloves, mask).
  • Consent: permission to help.
  • Vital signs: pulse (heartbeats), breathing rate, and skin condition.
  • Radial pulse: the pulse at the wrist. Carotid pulse: the pulse at the side of the neck.
  • ABCs: Airway, Breathing, Circulation (blood flow).
  • Ligament: tissue that holds bone to bone at a joint. Muscle moves the body.
  • Epidermis: the outer skin layer. Dermis: the layer under it.
  • Fracture: a broken bone. Splint / immobilize: keep it from moving.
  • Litter: a stretcher.
  • First aid = immediate treatment given to the injured or ill before a doctor or corpsman is available
  • Objectives:
    1. Ease pain
    2. Prevent further harm
    3. Extend life

Four life-saving steps

  1. Stop the bleeding: direct pressure, then elevation, then tourniquet (worst case only)
  2. Protect the wound: with a clean dressing
  3. Prevent/treat shock:
    • Lay the victim down with the head lower than the body, loosen tight clothing, and keep them warm
    • If unconscious, place them on their side
    • No fluids by mouth
  4. Restore breathing: start artificial respiration immediately

Roles of a first aider

  • Don't do the physician's job or compete with them
  • Your duty ends when a physician or more qualified responder arrives
  • Ensure the safety of yourself, your team and bystanders
  • Gain access to the victim and identify threats to life
  • Call for help, give care, assist advanced personnel, and record the situation

Characteristics of a good first aider: Gentle · Resourceful · Observant · Tactful · Empathetic · Respectable

  • ⚠️ Memory hook: "GROTER"

Hindrances: an unsafe scene, crowds (misleading information), victim or relatives (panic and pressure)

  • ⚠️ Safety percentage: first aider 80%, victim 20%

Emergency = a sudden onset of medical/surgical severity that could seriously endanger health without immediate attention.

Call First vs Care First

  • CALL FIRST = get advanced medical help first
  • CARE FIRST = give first aid first, then get help

Emergency Action Principles

1. Survey the scene

  • Is it safe? What happened? How many are hurt? Are there bystanders who can help?
  • Get consent: "I'm ___, trained first aider, I have my PPE on, may I help you?"
  • Check responsiveness:
    • Alertness: give a simple instruction (e.g. raise your hand)
    • Verbal: ask a simple question (e.g. their name)
    • Unresponsive: tap the shoulder 3 times: "Hey, hey, hey, are you OK?"

2. Activate medical assistance

  • Point to a bystander with your open hand, not a finger, identifying them by attire: "Sir/Ma'am in ___, please call an ambulance and come back to me ASAP." Use a modulated voice.
  • ALERT Center: 8352-5000 / 8292-1405
  • The caller reports:
    • What happened
    • Location
    • Number injured
    • Extent of injury and first aid given
    • Phone number they're calling from
  • The caller identifies themselves and hangs up LAST

3. Secondary assessment (done when the person is regaining consciousness)

  • SAMPLE interview:
    • Signs/Symptoms
    • Allergies
    • Medications
    • Past/present illness
    • Last oral intake
    • Events prior
  • Vital signs:
    • Pulse (radial or carotid): adult 60–100 bpm
    • Breathing: adult 12–16 breaths/min
    • Skin: color, temperature, moisture
  • Head-to-toe check for "DOTS": Deformity · Open wound · Tenderness · Swelling

Wounds

  • Wound = an injury that breaks the skin or membrane and usually damages tissue underneath

Closed wounds (skin intact)

  • Bruise: discolored skin from ruptured blood vessels
  • Swelling
  • Strain: pulled or stretched muscle
  • Sprain: twisted ligament, with no dislocation
  • Dislocation: bone ends forced out of the joint
  • Closed fracture
  • ⚠️ Strain = muscle; sprain = ligament

Minor closed wound care: RICE

  • Rest · Immobilize · Cold compress · Elevate 8–12 inches (above the heart)

Internal bleeding signs: bruises, a painful/rigid abdomen, vomiting or coughing up blood, black or bloody stools

Major internal bleeding care

  • Monitor ABCs (Airway, Breathing, Circulation)
  • Lay the victim on their LEFT side (so vomit drains and isn't inhaled)
  • Raise the legs 8–12 inches
  • Get medical help

Open wounds

  • Puncture: made by a sharp pointed object
  • Abrasion: scrape
  • Laceration: deep cut or tear
  • Avulsion: a body part torn off
  • Amputation: loss of a limb

Open wound care

  1. Control bleeding (direct pressure)
  2. Clean and cover
  3. Care for shock
  4. Call a physician

Fractures

  • Simple/Closed: no break in the skin
  • Compound/Open: a wound connects to the broken bone. It's "complicated" if vessels, nerves or muscles are also injured.
  • Treatment: dress the wound, then immobilize (splint)
  • Neck/back fracture:
    • Lay the patient on their back
    • Put a small pad under the back and the hollow of the neck
    • Prepare an improvised litter

Burns

  • Caused by heat, cold, electricity, chemicals, friction, or radiation

By degree

Degree Damage Signs Care
1st (superficial) Outer layer only Redness, mild swelling, pain. Heals without scarring. Cold water 10–45 min or cold wet cloths
2nd (partial thickness) Epidermis plus upper dermis Blisters, weeping, severe pain Cold water/wet pack, pain reliever, don't break blisters
3rd (full thickness) All layers into fat and muscle Gray-white, cherry red or black. No pain (nerves destroyed). No cold, no ointment. Sterile non-stick dressing (no plastic). Check ABCs. Must get medical attention.

By cause

  • Thermal: stop the burning. If there are flames, smother them or have the victim roll.
  • Chemical (acids, alkalis, organics):
    • Brush off dry powder BEFORE flushing, then flush with water
    • Remove contaminated clothing and jewelry
    • Cover with a dry sterile dressing
  • Electrical:
    • Scene safe first. Unplug or turn off the power.
    • Treat all wires as live
    • Don't touch the person if the source is live
    • Check ABCs and check for spinal injury if they fell
    • Treat for shock (legs up 8–12 inches)

4. BASIC BANDAGING

📘 Words you need to know

  • Triangular bandage: a big triangle-shaped cloth.
    • Apex = its top point.
    • Base = its long bottom edge.
    • Tails / ends = its two bottom corners.
  • Open phase: the triangle fully opened (not folded).
  • Cravat: the triangle folded into a strip. Broad = wide, semi-broad = medium, narrow = thin.
  • Square knot: a flat, secure knot (right over left, then left over right).
  • Sling: a bandage that holds up an injured arm.
  • Laceration: a deep cut.
  • Temple: the side of the forehead. Nape: the back of the neck.
  • Betadine: an antiseptic (germ-killing liquid).
  • Elastic bandage: a stretchy roll bandage. Anchor = the first turns that hold it in place.

Types of bandages

  • Triangular: sling, pad to control bleeding, burns, support/immobilize bone or joint injuries
  • Elastic: sprains/strains (even pressure limits swelling). Also used for amputations.
  • Gauze: a dressing to cover a wound and protect it from infection

Triangular bandage

  • Parts: Apex (the top point), Base (the long side), two Tails/Ends (the corners of the base), Sides, and Body (the center)
  • Folds (each is made from the previous one):
    1. Open phase
    2. Broad cravat
    3. Semi-broad cravat
    4. Narrow cravat
  • To fold: hold the apex and the opposite end, "clap", reach for the body with the hand holding the apex, and pull. Use the same hand every time.
  • To return to open phase: hold the apex and base and pull in opposite directions
  • ⚠️ If the sides are visible, the fold is improper
  • Square knot: right tail over left, then left over right (or the reverse). It secures the bandage.
  • Burns: apply a dressing first (parchment or wax paper)

  • Slings: keep half the hand visible and the wrist accessible so you can check P.M.S.:
    • Pulse: index and middle finger
    • Motor: can they move their fingers?
    • Sensory: pinch or tap

Elastic bandage

  • Hold the roll in the right hand with the loose end at the bottom. The first 2–3 turns overlap to secure it.
  • Anchoring: lay the end obliquely across the wrist, go around, fold the uncovered triangle, and cover it with the third turn
  • Fastening: finish with circular turns, then tie, pin, or tape

Types

  • Circular: covers cylindrical parts
  • Spiral: cylindrical parts, but a larger area
  • Oblique: holds thick or temporary dressings that need frequent changes

Techniques

  • Figure-of-eight (hand): anchor near the fingers, go across the back of the hand to the thumb, and finish at the wrist
  • Recurrent (hand): for an amputated area. Anchor at the wrist and fold back over the tip 4–5 times.
  • Figure-of-eight (foot): anchor above the ankle and leave the heel exposed

5. VICTIM TRANSFERRING TECHNIQUES

📘 Words you need to know

  • Victim / patient / casualty: the injured person.
  • Bearer / rescuer / first aider: the person carrying them.
  • Carry / assist: a method of moving the patient. "Assist" = helping them walk.
  • Ergonomics: lifting safely (use your legs, not your back).
  • Airway obstruction: something blocking the mouth or throat.
  • Emergency rescue = quick transfer from an unsafe place to a safe one. Indications:
    • Fire or explosion
    • Toxic gas or lack of oxygen
    • Traffic
    • Drowning
    • Electrocution
    • Collapsing walls
  • Transfer = moving from a safe place to a safer one
  • With no assistance, drag or pull the victim

Factors in choosing a method

  1. Nature and severity of the injury
  2. Size of the victim
  3. Physical ability of the first aider
  4. Number of personnel and equipment
  5. Evacuation route
  6. Distance
  7. Gender of the victim — ⚠️ the LAST consideration

Pointers during transfer

  • Keep the airway open and the bleeding controlled
  • Keep the victim in the correct position, checked regularly, with bandages in place
  • The method must be safe, comfortable and speedy
  • Move the body as one unit
  • The tallest bearer stays at the head
  • Use proper ergonomics: lift with your knees/legs, not your back

Common steps

  1. Survey the scene
  2. Introduce yourself and ask permission:
    • Conscious: ask the patient
    • Unconscious: ask relatives, then bystanders. If you're alone, still ask the patient, because ⚠️ hearing is the LAST sense to fade.
  3. Kneel with one knee at the shoulder and the other at the pelvis/side
  4. Cross the patient's hands over their chest and lift the knees
  5. Slide your hands in with the palm under the nape. Your legs should be at 90°.
  6. Check the airway. Remove solid obstructions with the scissors, forceps, or baby hook finger sweep. Absorb liquids with a clean cloth.

Extremity carry One rescuer holds the torso from behind, the other holds the knees
3–4 man Hammock carry Line up by height, tallest at the head. Count off "1, 2" and the 2s go to the other side, ⚠️ walking around the FEET, never over the head. Interlock wrists under the patient. Everything is on the Team Leader's command.
  • When walking together, always step off with the INNER foot first. The Team Leader gives the commands.

6. BASIC LIFE SUPPORT (BLS) / CPR

📘 Words you need to know

  • BLS (Basic Life Support): keeping someone alive (breathing and heartbeat) until medics arrive.
  • CPR (Cardiopulmonary Resuscitation): chest pushes (compressions) plus rescue breaths.
  • Compression: pushing down hard on the chest. Ventilation: giving a breath.
  • AED (Automated External Defibrillator): a machine that shocks the heart back to a normal rhythm.
  • Sternum: the breastbone in the middle of the chest.
  • Trapezius squeeze: pinching the muscle between the neck and shoulder. Sternal rub: rubbing the breastbone with your knuckles. Both test whether the person reacts to pain.
  • FBAO: Foreign-Body Airway Obstruction (choking on an object).
  • Heimlich maneuver / abdominal thrusts: pushing in and up on the stomach to make a choking person cough out the object.
  • DNAR (Do Not Attempt Resuscitation): the person's written wish not to get CPR.
  • BVM (Bag Valve Mask): a squeeze bag with a mask, used to give breaths.
  • Recovery position: lying on the side so they don't choke on vomit.
  • BLS = maintaining airway, breathing and circulation with no equipment except a protective device
  • It includes:
    • Recognizing sudden cardiac death, heart attack, stroke, and FBAO (foreign-body airway obstruction)
    • CPR
    • AED defibrillation
  • The Chain of Survival appears on the handout

Steps

1. Survey the scene

  • Safe? Get consent with the PPE line.
  • Check responsiveness:
    • Alertness
    • Verbal
    • Pain stimulus (trapezius squeeze or sternal rub). ⚠️ No sternal rub if they have a heart condition.
    • Unresponsive: tap 3 times

If unresponsive

  • Airway: clear any obstruction
    • Baby hook = index finger
    • Scissors = index + middle finger
    • Forceps = index finger + thumb
  • Check breathing and circulation together for 10 seconds. Use the carotid pulse with your index and middle fingers.
Pulse Breathing Action
+ + No CPR needed
+ − Choking: do the Heimlich / abdominal thrusts
− − Do CPR

2. Activate medical assistance: "...call an ambulance, bring an AED, and come back to me ASAP."

3. Perform CPR

  • Don't start if the person has a DNAR (Do Not Attempt Resuscitation) order or is decapitated
  • During compressions:
    • Show the front (inside) of your elbow
    • Push with your upper body
    • Watch the face
  • Don't:
    • Bend your elbows
    • Massage the chest
    • Rock your head
    • Bounce your hands
    • Change your hand position

When to STOP CPR

  1. Spontaneous signs of breathing and circulation
  2. Handover to a professional or advanced provider
  3. Operator exhausted
  4. A physician takes over
  5. The scene becomes unsafe

After CPR

  • Check for DOTS, then place the person in the recovery position:
    1. Near arm out at a right angle, palm up
    2. Far hand's back against their near cheek
    3. Bend the far knee to 90°
    4. Roll them toward you by the knee
    5. Tilt the head back, lift the chin, and check the airway
    6. Stay and monitor until help arrives

1. Basic Signal Communication

Voice radio (talking on the radio)

  • Also called Voice Radio (VR) / radio telephony. Used for short range.
  • Works by line-of-sight (no big obstacles between radios).
  • Keep the mic 2 inches from your mouth. Test signals: max 10 seconds.
  • Listen before you talk. Speak slowly and clearly.
  • Don't say your real name. Don't break radio silence (an order that nobody transmits).

Calling

  • Clear signal (good) = call once. Weak signal (bad) = call twice.
  • Free net = anyone can talk. Directed net = you need permission.
  • The NCS is the net's "admin." Stations answer in alphabetical order.

Radio words (prowords)

Word Means
ROGER I received it
WILCO I'll comply (received, understood, will do)
SAY AGAIN Repeat please
CORRECTION I made a mistake
DISREGARD THIS TRANSMISSION Ignore this message (while still sending)
RELAY / RELAY TO Pass it on
EXEMPT Everyone except ___
  • ⚠️ Never say "ROGER WILCO" together.
  • Break-in (interrupting) is not allowed during combat messages except to report enemy contact.
  • Precedence = how urgent a message is.

Semaphore (two flags held in the hands)

  • Flags are 15–18 inches square, like the OSCAR flag (or PAPA).
  • It's faster than flashing light, more secure (short range), and used when there's radio silence.
  • Numbers are spelled out. For double letters, drop your arms to the front position.
  • Flag JULIET = "semaphore message for all ships."
  • Codes:
    • SEM = semaphore message coming
    • K = go ahead / answer
    • IMI = repeat
    • AS = wait
  • Wheel circles: 1st A–G, 2nd H–N, 3rd O–U, 4th V–Z.

Flashing light

  • Directional = a searchlight aimed at one ship (long range; 12" and 24" lights).
  • Non-directional = yardarm blinkers that everyone can see.

Morse code

  • Dot = 1 unit, dash = 3. Gap between letters = 3, between words = 7.
  • There are 36 patterns (26 letters + 10 numbers). Learn it "by wholes."
  • SOS = ... --- ... · A = .- · E = . · T = -

2. Basic Survival

  • Survival = staying alive in the field with limited resources.

SURVIVAL acronym (8th TD version)

  1. S = Size up the situation
  2. U = Undue haste makes waste (don't rush)
  3. R = Remember where you are
  4. V = Vanquish fear and panic
  5. I = Improvise
  6. V = Value living ("get out alive")
  7. A = Act like the natives
  8. L = Learn basic survival skills

The 9th TD version ends with L = Live by your wits instead.

Jungle

  • Prepare for bed early (it gets dark fast).
  • Tuck your pants into your boots.
  • Don't camp in low areas (valleys, dry riverbeds) because they flood.

Food

  • Animal food has the most food value. Anything that swims, flies, crawls or creeps can be food.
  • All four-legged animals, all eggs and insect larvae = edible. Avoid toads.
  • Snake: cut off 6 inches from the head.

Traps

  • Snare = a noose (loop that tightens). Trap = something that closes or falls on the animal.
  • Locking loop = tightens as the animal struggles
  • Drag noose = for medium animals
  • Deadfall = a heavy log or rock falls on it
  • Treadle spring = for small animals on a trail

Water

  • You need at least 2 quarts a day. Your body is more than 3/4 fluid.
  • Boil 1 minute, OR add 8 drops of 2½% iodine per canteen and wait 10 minutes.
  • Rainwater caught in clean containers is safe without purifying.

Shelter

  • Protects you from sun, insects, wind, rain, heat/cold, and enemy observation.
  • Pick a site safe from flooding and erosion, near water.

Fire

  • Uses: warmth, drying, cooking, purifying water, signaling.
  • Tepee = cone shape, works even with wet wood
  • Lean-to = green stick at 30°, pointing in the direction of the wind
  • Cross-ditch = a cross 1 ft wide, 3 in deep, which gives air flow (draft)

3. Basic Life Support (BLS) / CPR

  • BLS = keeping someone breathing and their heart pumping, with no equipment except a face shield or mask.

Steps

  1. Is the scene safe?
  2. Ask permission: "I'm ___, trained first aider…"
  3. Check if they respond:
    • Give a simple instruction or ask a question.
    • Pain test (trapezius squeeze or sternal rub). ⚠️ No sternal rub if they have heart problems.
    • Tap the shoulder 3×: "Hey, hey, hey, are you OK?"
  4. Clear the airway if something's blocking it:
    • Baby hook = index finger
    • Scissors = index + middle finger
    • Forceps = index finger + thumb
  5. Check breathing and pulse (carotid, at the neck) for 10 seconds.

5. Victim Transferring Techniques

  • Emergency rescue = moving from unsafe → safe. Examples: fire, gas, traffic, drowning, electricity, collapsing walls.
  • Transfer = moving from safe → safer.
  • Alone with no help? Drag or pull them.

Choosing a method

  • Consider the injury, the victim's size, your strength, how many helpers you have, the route, and the distance.
  • ⚠️ Gender is the LAST thing considered.

Rules

  • Tallest rescuer at the head.
  • Move the body as one unit.
  • Lift with your legs, not your back.
  • Ask permission: if unconscious, ask relatives or bystanders. If you're alone, still ask the patient. ⚠️ Hearing is the last sense to go.

6. First Aid

  • First aid = immediate help before a doctor or corpsman arrives.
  • Goals:
    1. Ease pain
    2. Prevent more harm
    3. Extend life

4 life-saving steps

  1. Stop the bleeding (press, then raise, then tourniquet as a last resort)
  2. Protect the wound
  3. Treat shock (head lower than the body, keep warm, no drinks)
  4. Restore breathing

Good first aider: "GROTER"

  • Gentle
  • Resourceful (improvises)
  • Observant
  • Tactful (doesn't cause panic)
  • Empathetic (comforting)
  • Respectable

Other key points

  • ⚠️ Safety: 80% first aider, 20% victim. Your job ends when a doctor or more qualified person arrives.
  • Call First = call for help first. Care First = give first aid first.
  • The caller hangs up last. ALERT Center: 8352-5000.
  • SAMPLE questions:
    • Signs/symptoms
    • Allergies
    • Medicines
    • Past illness
    • Last meal or drink
    • Events before
  • Normal adult:
    • Pulse 60–100
    • Breathing 12–16 per minute
  • DOTS (head-to-toe check):
    • Deformity
    • Open wound
    • Tenderness
    • Swelling (doesn't show right away)

Wounds

  • Closed (skin not broken): bruise, swelling, strain = muscle, sprain = ligament, dislocation (bone out of the joint).
  • RICE for minor ones: Rest, Immobilize, Cold compress, Elevate 8–12 in.
  • Internal bleeding (black stool, vomiting blood): lay them on their LEFT side and raise the legs.
  • Open:
    • Puncture = pointed object
    • Abrasion = scrape
    • Laceration = deep cut
    • Avulsion = torn off
    • Amputation = limb lost

Fractures

  • Closed = skin intact. Open/compound = wound reaches the bone.
  • Splint it so it doesn't move.
  • Neck or back fracture: lay them on their back with a pad under the back and neck.

Burns by degree

Degree Looks like Do
1st Red, painful, no scar Cold water 10–45 min
2nd Blisters Cold water, don't pop the blisters
3rd Gray, white, red or black, no pain No cold water, no ointment, no plastic. Get a doctor.

Burns by cause

  • Chemical: brush off the powder first, then flush with water.
  • Electrical: turn off the power and treat every wire as live.

8. Plebe Knowledge

  • "What art thou?" → "SIR, I'M CADET PRIVATE ___ FROM PLV-NROTCU, SIR."

Standing at attention

  • Eyes look 5000 miles away
  • Toes 45° apart
  • Stomach in, chest out

Rules

  • Start and end with Sir/Ma'am. Move on the double.
  • Report 15 min early.
  • Late: 30 push-ups and say "Sir, I'll never be late, Sir."
  • After the punishment, say "Sir, I'll be more disciplined, Sir."
  • Walk on the officer's LEFT, because the RIGHT is the place of honor.
  • Stick to the wall in corridors.
  • The most senior in the group salutes. When in doubt, salute.
  • Obey, obey, and never complain.
