## 1. What Is History?

| Source | Definition |
|---|---|
| **General** | The **study and interpretation of past events**, especially those involving **humans, societies, and civilizations** |
| **Teodoro Agoncillo** | "A study of **man and his deeds** in relation to society, particularly emphasizing the **struggle for freedom and national identity**" |
| **Ambeth Ocampo** | History is a **narrative of the past that is relevant to the present**, combining scholarship with **storytelling** to make history accessible |
| **Zeus Salazar** (*Pantayong Pananaw*) | "Ang kasaysayan ay **salaysay na may saysay** sa sinasalaysayang grupo ng tao." (History is a **story that has meaning** for the group of people it is about) |

> **Memory trick:** **Salazar** = **salaysay** + **saysay** (story + meaning). **Pantayong Pananaw** = "**from us, for us**": Filipino history told by Filipinos, for Filipinos. **(added)**

### Elements of History
**Time · Evidence · Human Actions · Interpretations**

### Significance of History
1. To **understand human experiences**
2. To **learn from past successes and mistakes**
3. To **preserve cultural identity and collective memory**

### History and Psychology (added)
Both study **human behavior**. Psychology explains **why** people act; history shows **how** they acted in the past. History helps explain a people's **values, trauma, and identity** (e.g., colonial mentality), while psychology helps interpret **motives** behind historical events.

## 2. Historiography

**Historiography** is the **study of how history is written**: the **methods, interpretations, sources, and perspectives** historians use.

> **Watch out:** **History** = the past itself / the study of it. **Historiography** = the study of **how** that history was **written**.

### Historical Sources

| Primary sources | Secondary sources |
|---|---|
| **Firsthand**, made **during** the time studied | **Secondhand**, made **later**, interpreting primary sources |
| **Fossils, artifacts, letters, journals, personal accounts, laws/legal documents, photographs, paintings, carvings** | **Books, articles, columns, news, documentaries/films** |

> **Own example:** Rizal's actual letter to his family = **primary**. A YouTube documentary about Rizal = **secondary**.

### Types of Historical Research

| Type | Focus (added) |
|---|---|
| **Narrative** | **Tells the story** of events in order |
| **Analytical (Interpretive)** | Explains **why** events happened (causes and effects) |
| **Comparative** | **Compares** events, societies, or periods |
| **Social** | Everyday life of **ordinary people** and social groups |
| **Intellectual** | History of **ideas**, beliefs, and thinkers |
| **Cultural** | **Customs, arts, traditions**, and values |
| **Economic and Political** | **Trade, wealth, government, and power** |

### Models of History (Mga Modelo ng Kasaysayan)

| Model | Meaning | Shape |
|---|---|---|
| **1. Linear** (also **"Providential"** or **"Fatalistic"**) | History has a **definite beginning and end**. Example: **the Bible** | A straight line ━ |
| **2. Cyclical** | History **returns to its start / repeats**: **"history repeats itself."** Example: **dynasties and kingdoms** rising and falling | A circle ○ |
| **3. Spiral** | A **critique of the cyclical** model: history **doesn't repeat exactly**, there are only **similarities**; aspects of time are **not** fully repeated | A spiral 🌀 |
| **4. Chaotic View** | History has **no pattern** and there's **no real history**. Inspired by **postmodernist** thought: history **can't be objective** because you can't know what's true, and no single discipline can cover everything | A tangled scribble |

> **Own example:** "Every few decades there's another pandemic, but each one is different" = **spiral**. "Empires always rise and fall the same way" = **cyclical**.

### Schools of Thought in History (added: slides only name them)

| School | Idea |
|---|---|
| **Positivism** | History must be based on **facts and documents** ("**no document, no history**"). Aims to be **objective and scientific** |
| **Postcolonialism** | Re-examines history **from the view of the colonized**, challenging accounts written by **colonizers**. Aims to recover the **native perspective** |

> **Link:** *Pantayong Pananaw* fits **postcolonialism**: telling our history from our own point of view.

> **Mnemonic by age (oldest → newest):** **H**omo luzonensis → **T**abon → **A**ngono petroglyphs → **M**anunggul → **B**alangay → **G**olden Tara / **L**aguna Copperplate = "**H**appy **T**eachers **A**lways **M**ake **B**etter **G**rades **L**ater"

### Fossils vs Artifacts (added)

| | Fossil | Artifact |
|---|---|---|
| What it is | Preserved **remains or traces of a once-living thing** (bones, teeth, shells, footprints) | An **object made or changed by humans** (tools, jars, jewelry, carvings, writing) |
| Made by | **Nature** | **People** |
| Examples here | **Homo luzonensis** bones and teeth, **Tabon Man** skullcap | **Manunggul Jar**, **Bulul**, **Golden Tara**, **Laguna Copperplate**, **Balangay** |

> **Memory trick:** **F**ossil = once **f**lesh (it was alive). **Art**ifact = made by human **art**/skill. Both are **primary sources**.

### Origin of Filipinos: Austronesian Origin
- Filipinos are part of the **Austronesian** peoples, who spread by **sea** from **Taiwan (~3000 BC)** to the **Philippines (~2000 BC)**, then to Indonesia, Micronesia, Melanesia, Polynesia (Hawaii, Easter Island, New Zealand), and even **Madagascar**.
- Prof. Padilla: *"Kung nanood po kayo ng **Moana**, 'yun ang lahi natin."* (Moana is Polynesian, a fellow Austronesian people.)
- The slides also included a **fact-check of Sen. Robinhood Padilla's "Luzvimindan Project"** claim, as a lesson on **verifying sources**.

## 5. Pre-Colonial Society

### Social Classes

```
RULING CLASS ─── Datu / Rajah / Sultan
NOBLE CLASS ──── Maharlika (Tagalog warriors)
            └─── Timawa (Visayan warriors/commoners)
COMMONERS ────── Aliping Namamahay
            └─── Uripon (Visayan equivalent)
DEPENDENT CLASS ─ Aliping Saguiguilid
```

| Class | Description (added details) |
|---|---|
| **Datu / Rajah / Sultan** | **Rulers** of the barangay/territory |
| **Maharlika** | **Tagalog warrior** nobles who fought for the datu |
| **Timawa** | **Visayan** free people / warriors |
| **Aliping Namamahay** | Had **their own house** and family; served the master but kept some property |
| **Uripon** | **Visayan** term for dependents/servants |
| **Aliping Saguiguilid** | Lived **in the master's house**, fully dependent, the **lowest** class |

> **Memory trick:** **Namamahay** = may **bahay** (has a house). **Saguiguilid** = sa **gilid** (lives at the side of the master's house).

### Belief System and Values

| Belief | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Animism** | Belief that **nature and objects** (rivers, trees, mountains, animals) have **spirits** (**anito** or **diwata**) |
| **Polytheism** | Worship of **many gods and goddesses**, each tied to an aspect of life |

**Gods (Polytheism)**

| God | Role |
|---|---|
| **Bathala** | **Tagalog supreme deity** |
| **Kaptan** | **Visayan sky god** |
| **Amanikable** | **Tagalog sea god** |
| **Lakapati** | **Tagalog fertility goddess** |

**Spiritual leaders**
- **Babaylan** (usually **female**) / **Katalonan** (**Tagalog** term): **shamans** who performed **rituals, healing, and communication with spirits**.
- **Bagani** (**warrior class**) also had spiritual roles in some areas.
- Artwork shown: **"Evolution of Medicine"** by **Carlos "Botong" Francisco**.

**Filipino values from pre-colonial times (added examples):** **bayanihan** (community cooperation), **respect for elders**, **hospitality**, **utang na loob** (debt of gratitude), close **family ties**, respect for **nature**.

### Indigenous Peoples (IPs)
Groups who have **preserved their distinct cultures, languages, traditions, and ancestral lands** from pre-colonial times to today, often living **outside mainstream lowland, urban society**.

| Region | Groups |
|---|---|
| **Luzon** | **Igorot, Aeta** |
| **Visayas** | **Ati** |
| **Mindanao** | **Lumad** |

## 6. The Rise of Islam in the Philippines

### What Is Islam?
- **Islam** = **"submission to the will of God."**
- Began in **Mecca, Saudi Arabia**.
- **Prophet Muhammad** received revelations through the angel **Jibreel (Gabriel)**.
- God: **Allah**.
- Led after Muhammad by the **Caliphate system** (caliphs).
- The first revealed words (Qur'an 96): *"**Read** in the name of your Lord who created — created man from a clot of blood..."*

### The 4 Caliphs (Rightly Guided)
1. **Abu Bakr**: first caliph
2. **Umar**: father-in-law of Muhammad
3. **Uthman**: son-in-law of the Prophet
4. **Ali**: cousin and son-in-law of the Prophet

> **Mnemonic:** **A**ll **U**mbrellas **U**sually **A**rrive = Abu Bakr, Umar, Uthman, Ali

### Sunni vs Shia

| | **Sunni** | **Shia** |
|---|---|---|
| Followers | **85–90%** of Muslims | **10–15%** of Muslims |
| Origin of split | Successor chosen by **consensus (community)** | Leadership should stay in the **Prophet's family**, starting with **Ali** |
| First leader after Muhammad | **Abu Bakr** (companion) | **Ali ibn Abi Talib** (cousin and son-in-law) |
| Leader title | **Caliph**: political and religious leader **chosen by people** | **Imam**: spiritual and political leader, **from the Prophet's bloodline** |
| Strongholds | Egypt, Turkey, Saudi Arabia, **Indonesia**, most of the Muslim world | **Iran, Iraq**, Bahrain, Azerbaijan, parts of Lebanon, Yemen, Pakistan, India |

> **Memory trick:** **Sh**ia = **sh**ares the **bloodline** (family). **Sunni** = **s**elected by the **s**ociety.

### How Islam Spread in the Philippines
1. **13th century**: Islam arrives through **trade**
2. **Establishment** of Islam in present-day **BARMM**
3. **Sultanates of Sulu and Maguindanao**
4. **Influence of Islam in Luzon and Manila**

### Key People and Sultanates

| Place | Person | Year | Key facts |
|---|---|---|---|
| **Tawi-Tawi** | **Sheikh Karim ul-Makhdum** | **1380** | Built the **oldest mosque** in the Philippines: **Sheikh Karimul Makhdum Mosque, Simunul, Tawi-Tawi** |
| **Sulu** | **Rajah Baguinda** | **1390** | **Missionary leader from Indonesia** (Sumatra); strengthened Islam and political structure in Sulu; one of the **founding fathers** of Islam here |
| **Sulu Sultanate** | **Sharif ul-Hashim** | **~1450** | **First Sultan of Sulu**; a **descendant of Prophet Muhammad** ("Sharif"); linked Sulu to **Muslim trade networks** |
| **Maguindanao Sultanate** | **Sharif Kabungsuan** | **1515** | **Missionary from Malaysia** (Johor); arrived in **Cotabato**; **founder of the Sultanate of Maguindanao** |
| **Manila** | **Rajah Sulaiman, Rajah Matanda, Lakan Dula** | **~1500** | **Early Muslim** rulers in Manila/Tondo |

> **Timeline mnemonic:** **1380** Makhdum (mosque) → **1390** Baguinda → **1450** Sulu Sultanate → **1500** Manila rajahs → **1515** Maguindanao

### The Five Pillars of Islam

| Pillar | Meaning |
|---|---|
| **Shahadah** | **Faith**: declaring there is no god but Allah and Muhammad is His messenger |
| **Salah** | **Prayer**: **five times a day** (added) |
| **Sawm** | **Fasting**: during **Ramadan** (added) |
| **Zakah** | **Almsgiving**: giving to the poor |
| **Hajj** | **Pilgrimage** to **Mecca**, at least once if able |

> **Mnemonic:** **S**ome **S**tudents **S**tudy **Z**ealously **H**ere = Shahadah, Salah, Sawm, Zakah, Hajj

### Datu Puti and the 10 Bornean Datus
- A **legend**: ten datus led by **Datu Puti** fled **Borneo** and sailed to **Panay**. (added) They "bought" Panay from the **Ati** chief **Marikudo** with a **golden salakot** (the **"Barter of Panay"**, shown in the slide painting).
- Panay was divided into three districts: **Hantik (Antique)**, **Irong-Irong (Iloilo)**, and **Aklan**.

### Conflicting Views in Philippine History

| Claim | Status |
|---|---|
| **Datu Puti and the 10 Bornean Datus** | **Legend**: from the *Maragtas* (by **Pedro Monteclaro**, 1907) (added), not proven by historical evidence |
| **Code of Kalantiaw** | **Hoax**: a fake "pre-colonial legal code" created by **Jose E. Marco** in **1913** (added); exposed by historian **William Henry Scott** |

> **Lesson:** Always check whether a "historical" source is **primary, reliable, and verified**, as in the Kalantiaw hoax and the Padilla fact-check.

## 7. Magellan's Expedition

### Age of Exploration
- Europeans sailed in search of new routes and lands, driven by the **3 Gs: God, Gold, and Glory** (spread **Christianity**, gain **wealth/spices**, win **fame and power**). **(added meaning)**
- **Treaty of Tordesillas (1494)**: drew the **Line of Demarcation** that split the newly explored world between **Spain (west)** and **Portugal (east)**. (added) The slide map also shows the **Treaty of Zaragoza (1529)**, the matching line on the other side of the globe.

The slide's five events: **Age of Exploration (God, Gold, Glory) → Magellan's Expedition → Battle of Mactan → First Circumnavigation → Christianity and the First Catholic Mass**.

### Key People

### Battle of Mactan
- **Lapu-Lapu**, datu of **Mactan**, refused to recognize **Humabon** and the King of Spain.
- **April 27, 1521** (added): Magellan attacked with a small force and was **overwhelmed and killed** by Lapu-Lapu's warriors.
- Considered the **first successful Filipino resistance** against foreign invaders. **(added)**
- "**Sinong pumatay kay Lapu-Lapu?**" is a trick question from the slides. **Lapu-Lapu's side killed Magellan**, not the other way around; how Lapu-Lapu himself died is **not recorded**.

### Moluccas (Spice Islands)
- The **real goal** of the expedition: a **westward route** to the **Moluccas**, in present-day **Indonesia**. **(added)**
- Source of **cloves, nutmeg, and mace**, which were worth a fortune in Europe. **(added)**
- The survivors reached **Tidore** in the Moluccas in **November 1521**, after Magellan's death.

## 8. Las Islas Filipinas

### The Next Expeditions

| Expedition | Years | Key facts | Outcome |
|---|---|---|---|
| **García Jofre de Loaísa** | **1525–1536** | Followed Magellan's route to reach the **Spice Islands**; many died of **scurvy**, including Loaísa; survivors reached the **Moluccas** | **Failed** to establish a settlement |
| **Ruy López de Villalobos** | **1542–1546** | Arrived in **Mindanao** in **1543**; **named the islands "Las Islas Filipinas"** in honor of **Prince Philip of Spain** (later **King Philip II**); tried to colonize Leyte and Mindanao | **Abandoned** for lack of food and conflict with the Portuguese; Villalobos died in the Moluccas |
| **Miguel López de Legazpi** | **1564–1565** | With navigator **Andrés de Urdaneta**; arrived **February 13, 1565**; founded the **first permanent Spanish settlement in Cebu (1565)**; moved to **Manila in 1571** and made it the capital | **Start of Spanish colonization**, which lasted **over 300 years** |

> **Mnemonic:** **L**ittle **V**illages **L**ast = **L**oaísa, **V**illalobos, **L**egazpi. **Villalobos named it, Legazpi claimed it.**

### Legazpi and Urdaneta

| | **Miguel López de Legazpi** | **Andrés de Urdaneta** |
|---|---|---|
| Role (slide) | **Politics and Military** | **Religion (Augustinian) and Navigation** |
| Who (added) | Leader of the expedition; **first Governor-General** of the Philippines; founded **Cebu (1565)** and **Manila (1571)** | **Augustinian friar** and navigator; found the **return route (*tornaviaje*)** from the Philippines to **Mexico**, which made the **Manila–Acapulco Galleon Trade** possible |

> **Memory trick:** **Legazpi = sword** (rule and conquest). **Urdaneta = cross and compass** (friar and navigator).

### Bohol → Cebu → Manila

| Place | Event |
|---|---|
| **Bohol** | **Blood Compact (*Sandugo*)** in **Tagbilaran, Bohol** between **Legazpi** and **Rajah Sikatuna**; painted by **Juan Luna**. (added: **March 16, 1565**; the painting is *El Pacto de Sangre*, 1886; a blood compact sealed **friendship** by drinking wine mixed with each other's blood) |
| **Cebu** | **Rajah Tupas** resisted Legazpi, then reached a **peace agreement on June 4, 1565**. **Fort San Pedro** was built: the **first military barracks and seat of government under Spanish colonial rule** |
| **Manila** | **Martin de Goiti** and **Juan de Salcedo** conquered Manila in **1571** in the **Battle of Bangkusay** |

### Battle of Bangkusay
- **1571** (added: **June 3, 1571**, in **Bangkusay Channel, Tondo**).
- Spaniards under **Martin de Goiti** defeated a native fleet led by the **"King of Macabebe"** (shown on the slide). (added) He is known as **Tarik Sulayman**, a **Kapampangan** chief who was killed in the battle and is remembered as one of the **first Filipinos to die for freedom**.
- Result: the **fall of Manila** to Spain. (added) Legazpi made Manila the **capital** on **June 24, 1571**.
- (added) **Juan de Salcedo** was **Legazpi's grandson**. Don't confuse the King of Macabebe with **Rajah Sulaiman of Manila**.

### Intramuros and Fort Santiago (added details)
- **Intramuros** = "**within the walls**": the **walled city** of Manila, center of Spanish government and religion.
- **Fort Santiago**: the citadel at the mouth of the **Pasig River** inside Intramuros; **Rizal was imprisoned there** before his execution.

### Spanish Colonial Rule: Three Systems

| | **Encomienda System** | **Reduccion** | **Polo y Servicio** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Introduced | **1565** by **Legazpi** | **Late 1500s** by **missionaries and officials** | **1589** under **Gov. Gen. Santiago de Vera** |
| How it worked | The King granted an **encomienda (territory)** to a Spaniard (**encomendero**). Natives there paid **tribute** in cash, goods (rice, wax, chickens), or labor | Scattered barangays were **forcibly resettled** into centralized towns (**pueblos**) around a **plaza complex** (church, convent, town hall, market) | All **Filipino males 16–60** gave **40 days of forced labor** a year (later **15 days**) |
| Purpose | Supposed to **protect natives and teach Christianity**, but abused for **profit** | Easier **taxation, labor collection, and religious instruction** | **Shipbuilding (galleons)**, roads and bridges, churches, military service, logging |
| Impact | Foundation of **economic exploitation** and early colonial governance | Destroyed pre-colonial settlements; created the **barrio/town system** still seen today | **Suffering and depopulation**, but also **infrastructure** |

> **Memory trick:** **Encomienda = tribute** (pay). **Reduccion = relocation** (move "**under the bells**," *bajo de la campana*) **(added)**. **Polo = forced labor** (work). (added) A man could skip polo by paying the ***falla***.

### The Natives' 3 Options
**To Submit · To Fight · To Flight.** Those who **fled to the mountains** became our remaining **Indigenous Peoples** today.

## 9. GomBurZa

**GomBurZa** = **Gom**ez + **Bur**gos + **Za**mora: three **Filipino secular priests** executed by **garrote** on **February 17, 1872**. (The slide opens with the "**MaJoHa**" quiz-show meme: the correct answer is **GomBurZa**.)

> The slides for this topic are **question prompts and pictures only**. The answers below come from the **GomBurZa documentary** watched in class. Items marked **(added)** are not in the documentary. The documentary spells the name "**Gomes**"; most textbooks use "**Gomez**."

### Before 1872: Revolts That Were Not United
- Earlier revolts (**Gabriela Silang, Palaris, Tamblot**) were **separate, not united**, which is why they were always defeated.
- The earliest revolts had a **religious orientation**: **Bancao (Leyte), Sumuroy (Samar), Tapar (Panay)**, all in the **17th century**. Many leaders were **babaylans or their children** who wanted to return to the **original religion**.
- **Hermano Pule** continued that tradition.

### Hermano Pule
- **Apolinario de la Cruz** of **Tayabas** (now Quezon).
- Founded the **Cofradía de San José**, whose members spread through the **Tagalog regions**.
- The **friars saw it as a competitor** and moved to persecute it. The members resisted and even **won one battle**, but were then **massacred** in **1841**.
- Hermano Pule was **beheaded and his body dismembered**.
- **Tayabas Regiment revolt (January 19–20, 1843)**: soldiers from Tayabas, angered by what was done to him, **attacked Fort Santiago** shouting "**independence**." They failed, but it showed an aspiration to **cut ties with Spain**.
- **Link to GomBurZa:** documents suggest Hermano Pule had dealings with **Fr. Pedro Pelaez**, a pioneer of **secularization**.
- (added) He had wanted to be a priest but was **rejected by the religious orders because he was an *indio***, and the Cofradía accepted **natives only**.

> **Chain of religious struggle:** **Bancao → Sumuroy → Tapar → Hermano Pule → GomBurZa** (and it did not end there).

### The World Around 1872

| Year | Event | Effect |
|---|---|---|
| **1565–1815** | **Galleon Trade** (started by **Legazpi**; ended as **Mexico** moved toward independence) | The Philippines was ruled by Spain **through Mexico** |
| **1834** | Philippines **opened to world trade** | Foreign traders entered; some *indios* grew **rich as middlemen** and could **study at UST** |
| **1868** | **Glorious Revolution** in Spain: the **monarchy fell**, replaced by a **republican government** | A **liberal** governor-general was sent |
| **1869** | **Suez Canal** opened (plus **steamships**) | Faster travel and the **quick entry of liberal ideas**: **Voltaire, Rousseau, John Locke**, the **French and American Revolutions** |

- By this time Spain had only **four colonies** left: **Cuba, Puerto Rico, Guam, and the Philippines**.
- The real problem of educated Filipinos was **not poverty but lack of honor**: even with an education, they were still **looked down on** as *indios*.

### Frailocracy
- **Frailocracy** = rule of the friars. **Marcelo H. del Pilar** called it "**monastic supremacy**."
- Civil officials (governors-general) stayed only about **three years**; a friar stayed in one town for **40 years or more**. Through **confession** he knew everyone's secrets, which gave him **power**.
- Friars owned **haciendas**, which gave them **financial and political power**, not just spiritual.
- **Land grabbing** in **Cavite** displaced many people, who became **bandits** to get justice. Cavite was called "***La Madre de los Ladrones***" (**mother of thieves**). **Dr. Isagani Medina** (*Cavite Before the Revolution*) showed they were "lawless" **because the friars took their land**. Example: the **Silang uprising of 1745**.
- Oppressive policies: **polo y servicios, bandala, encomienda, hacienda system**.

### Two Governors-General

| | **Carlos María de la Torre** | **Rafael de Izquierdo** |
|---|---|---|
| Term | **1869–1871** | From **1871** (added: until 1873) |
| Sent because | The **Glorious Revolution** put a **republican** government in Spain | The republic fell and the **monarchy was restored** |
| Style | **Liberal**, open-minded | **Very strict**; ruled with an "**iron hand**" and disliked liberals |
| Known for | Tried to **win the hearts** of liberal Filipinos (priests, lawyers, businessmen); a **friend of Burgos**; gave **exemption privileges** | **Removed the privileges** (exemption from tribute and forced labor) of the Cavite arsenal workers |

> **Memory trick:** **De la Torre = tolerant.** **Izquierdo = iron hand.**
> **Link to "Queen Isabel II" (added):** she was the monarch **overthrown in the 1868 Glorious Revolution**. This is the era she actually belongs to.

### Regular vs Secular Priests

| | **Regular priests (friars)** | **Secular priests** |
|---|---|---|
| Meaning | "**Friar**" = **brother**; "regular" from ***regla*** (rule): **bound by the rules** of an order | Priests **under a bishop / diocese** |
| Orders | **Dominicans, Augustinians, Recollects, Franciscans** | None |
| Vows | **Poverty, celibacy, obedience** to the head of the order | — |
| Who | **Peninsulares** (full-blooded Spaniards born in Spain) | Other classes: ***indios*, mestizos, *insulares*** |
| Job (Council of Trent) | **Mission work and conversion** only | **Run the parishes** |

- The **Jesuits** have a **fourth vow: obedience to the Pope**, so they can claim they are **not friars**.
- The friars refused to obey the bishop ("our loyalty is to our order"), so there was an **ecclesiastical clash**.
- At first the regulars held the parishes only because there were **too few secular priests**. Later they **refused to give them up**, because **a parish is power**.

### Secularization
**Secularization** = the regular priests should **turn over the parishes to the secular priests**, whose job it really was.

**The Jesuit domino effect:**
1. **1768**: the King of Spain quarreled with the Pope and **expelled the Jesuits**.
2. Jesuit parishes went to the **Recollects**; the Recollects' parishes went to the **seculars**. The seculars finally had parishes.
3. **1859**: the **Jesuits returned** and took back their parishes (especially in **Mindanao**) from the Recollects.
4. The Recollects then **took back** the parishes held by the seculars. **The seculars lost.**
5. The **call for secularization intensified**.

**Why it was blocked:**
- **Racism**: "**purity of blood**." Spaniards saw themselves as superior, mestizos as lower, *indios* lowest.
- **Fear of revolution**: in **Mexico (1810)** the independence revolt was led by a priest, **Fr. Miguel Hidalgo**; Mexico became independent in **1821**. Spain feared Filipino priests would do the same.

**Leaders:**
- **Fr. Pedro Pelaez**: the **forerunner** of the movement and **mentor of Burgos**. He died in the **1863 earthquake**, crushed when the **Manila Cathedral** fell.
- **Burgos** then led the movement, with **Gomez** and **Zamora**.

> **Significance:** secularization became a fight for **equality** between Filipino and Spanish priests, and it led directly to the events of 1872.

### The Three Priests

| | **Mariano Gomez** | **Jacinto Zamora** | **Jose Burgos** |
|---|---|---|---|
| Age in 1872 | **72** (the **oldest**; born 1799) | **36** | **35** (the **youngest**) |
| From | **Sta. Cruz, Manila** | **Pandacan, Manila** | **Vigan, Ilocos**; a ***Criollo*** (Spanish parents), orphaned and sent to Manila |
| Parish | **Bacoor, Cavite**, since **1824** (**48 years**) | **Marikina** (also Batangas, Pasig); with Burgos at the **Manila Cathedral** | **Manila Cathedral** |
| Known for | "**One-man NGO**": built roads, lent capital for **salt farms**; **mediator** who convinced the rebel **Luis Parang** to come down, weakening banditry in Cavite | **Brilliant**: top three in the 1863/64 exams with Burgos (his classmate); his mother wanted him to be a **lawyer** | **Seven degrees, two doctorates**; a **teacher** (Paciano Rizal's); friend of **de la Torre**; a **pioneer anthropologist**; leader of secularization |
| Why implicated | Partner of **Pelaez** in the movement | A letter saying to bring "**bullet and gunpowder**," which was **gambling code for money** | Named by **Zaldua** as the priest who **paid the mutineers** |
| At the execution | **Calm and ready** | Had **lost his mind**; quiet and stunned | **Crying**, asking **why he had to die** when he was innocent |

- All three studied at **San Juan de Letran**, then **UST**, then the seminary.
- **Burgos's message:** Filipinos are **capable**, and discrimination against them should end.

### The Committee of Reformers and the Students
- **Committee of Reformers**: priests (including **Gomez** and **Zamora**), **businessmen, lawyers, and journalists** pushing for reforms.
- Their followers were **UST students**. In the "**demonstration of 1869**" they spread leaflets saying *indios* should **not be insulted by their professors**. It was one of the **first student movements** in the Philippines.
- The students: **Maximo Paterno, Paciano Rizal, Felipe Buencamino, Ambrosio Rianzares Bautista** (who later **read the Declaration of Independence**).
- The Spaniards suspected **Burgos** was behind it because he was the students' **idol**.

**Paciano Rizal and Felipe Buencamino** were both student activists who looked up to Burgos; **Paciano was Burgos's student**. They would later be part of the **Propaganda Movement and the Revolution**.

### The Cavite Mutiny
- **January 20, 1872**, at the Spanish arsenal in **Fort San Felipe, Cavite City**.
- Filipino **soldiers** at the arsenal revolted after being **stripped of their privileges**, led by **Sgt. La Madrid**. It involved **soldiers only**, not civilians.
- They took control and killed Spaniards, but **reinforcements crushed** them. The mutineers were **shot while surrendering**. People who were **not part of the mutiny** were arrested too.

| View | Claim |
|---|---|
| **Pardo de Tavera** | It was only a **localized event** in Cavite |
| **Spanish government** | A plot to **topple the whole government**, funded by **Filipino liberals** |
| **Fr. John Schumacher** (Jesuit historian) | Documents show a **bigger conspiracy**: a mutiny was also planned in **Manila**, but **Izquierdo received a letter** revealing the plot |

- The real **masterminds**, according to the documents: **Maximo Inocencio, Crisanto de los Reyes, and Enrique Paraiso**, all **masons**. They were only **exiled to the Marianas (Guam)**.
- **Schumacher's theory:** Izquierdo was a **mason** too, so he spared his fellow masons and the **blame was shifted to the three priests**.

### What Was the Evidence?
- **Francisco Zaldua**, a mutineer, testified that a priest **paid the mutineers** and named **Burgos**, calling himself Burgos's **messenger**. He **made it up to save himself**, but he was **executed anyway**.
- **Zamora's letter** about "bullet and gunpowder" (a gambling invitation).
- **No document has ever surfaced** proving the priests were the brains of the mutiny. They were **just implicated**.
- The trial was a **military trial**, finished in **less than a month**, and its **documents have never been found**. It was a **mockery of a trial**.

### The Verdict
- **Sentenced to death.** They were held in **Fort Santiago**, then moved to the cuartel in **Bagumbayan** the day before.
- **Archbishop Meliton Martinez** refused Izquierdo's demand to **strip them of their priesthood**. He said they would die **wearing their habits** and that he would **toll the bells** for them. This meant he **believed they were innocent**.
- **February 17, 1872**, at **Bagumbayan** (now **Luneta**), by **garrote** (an iron collar tightened by a screw until the spine broke).
- Order: **Zaldua → Gomez → Zamora → Burgos**.
- The crowd **knelt and prayed**, especially at Burgos's turn (account of the Frenchman **Edmund Plauchut**).
- They were buried in **Paco Cemetery**.

**Last words:**
- **Gomez**, when arrested: "**I will come back.**" Asked to choose a confessor: get "**the priest who hates us the most, so they would know how clean our conscience is.**"
- **Burgos**: "**Go to the museums and libraries in other countries, so you will know who your ancestors are.**"

> **Mnemonic (order of execution):** **Z**aldua **G**oes, **Z**amora **B**reaks = **Z**aldua, **G**omez, **Z**amora, **B**urgos.

### Paciano and Rizal
- **Paciano** was Burgos's student and was very close to his brother **Jose (Pepe)**, so he likely **influenced Rizal** about the execution.
- After 1872 Filipinos **fell silent**; the one who dared mention the priests was **Rizal**, who **dedicated *El Filibusterismo*** to them.
- (added) Jose was only **10** in 1872, and he used the surname **Rizal** instead of **Mercado** so he would not be linked to Paciano and Burgos.

### Why 1872 Matters
- Filipinos could not accept that Spaniards would **kill priests**. It showed there was **something wrong with the system**.
- **Teodoro Agoncillo**: "the Philippines **had no history before 1872**." He meant that before then people saw themselves as **Bicolano, Ilocano, Tagalog, Kapampangan, Bisaya**, not **Filipino**. 1872 was the **start of national consciousness**.
- **Emilio Jacinto** wrote an essay titled *Gomes, Burgos, at Zamora*; "**Gomburza**" became a **password of the Katipunan**, which said it was **avenging** the three priests.

> **Chain to remember:** **GomBurZa (1872)** → **Propaganda Movement** (Rizal, del Pilar, Lopez Jaena, Ponce: reforms only) → **La Liga Filipina** → Rizal exiled to **Dapitan** → **Katipunan** (Bonifacio) → **Philippine Revolution** → **Independence, 1898** (Aguinaldo).
